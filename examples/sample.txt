The Industrial Revolution was a period of major industrialization and innovation
that began in Britain in the late 1700s and spread across the world through
the 1800s. New manufacturing processes replaced hand production with machines,
powered first by water and then by steam. The textile industry was transformed
by inventions like the spinning jenny and the power loom, while advances in
iron production and the steam engine enabled railways and steamships that
connected distant markets.

Urbanization accelerated as workers moved from rural areas to factory towns,
dramatically increasing city populations. This migration created crowded living
conditions and long working hours, but also rising wages and new economic
opportunities over time. Child labor was widespread in early factories until
reform laws gradually restricted it.

Economically, the Industrial Revolution shifted societies from agrarian
economies to industrial ones, raising overall productivity and living standards
in the long run. It also widened inequality in the short term, as factory
owners accumulated wealth while workers faced harsh conditions. Politically,
the era fueled labor movements, trade unions, and debates about the role of
government in regulating industry — debates that shaped modern economic policy.

Technologically, the period laid the foundation for the modern world:
interchangeable parts, mass production, the telegraph, and early electrical
engineering all trace their roots to this era. Historians generally agree that
while the transition caused real hardship, it ultimately enabled the sustained
economic growth that defines the contemporary global economy.
